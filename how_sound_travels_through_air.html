How sound travels through air

A vibrating source pushes and pulls on the air particles next to it. What moves outwards is the pattern of squashed and spread-out air. This is energy being transferred from one particle to another, eventually reaching your eardrum.

With (voiced) speech sounds, the lungs build up pressure below the closed vocal folds. That pressure forces the folds apart, a little puff of air escapes, and the folds snap shut again. This repeats very fast. Each puff is one push, so it creates one compression, just like one push of the speaker cone in the animation. (This is a simplification of what's known as the Bernoulli effect)

The number of puffs ('glottal pulses') per second is the fundamental frequency, which we hear as pitch: very roughly 100 a second for a typical male voice and 200 for a typical female voice.

Particles stay putWatch the orange particles. Each one wobbles around its own resting place (the small ring) and never travels with the wave. The energy is passed along from particle to particle.
Compression and rarefactionWhere particles bunch up, air pressure is higher (compression). Where they spread out, it is lower (rarefaction). The graph traces these ups and downs. This is the same wave shape we draw for sound.
Pitch and loudnessRaising the pitch makes the speaker vibrate more often, so compressions are packed closer together (shorter wavelength). Raising the loudness makes each push bigger, so particles move further and the pressure swings are larger but the rate of swings stays the same. Try changing one while the wave is moving.
